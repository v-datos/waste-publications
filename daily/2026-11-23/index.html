The takeaway Consignaction's new survey release shifts Québec's deposit-return debate toward a measurement question: satisfaction among nearby users and the incidence of service problems answer different questions.

At a glance

  • Consignaction reports strong intentions to return, but its survey screens for proximity and recent participation.
  • Lethbridge announced an exceptional weekend outlet for snow-damaged branches after seasonal depots closed.
  • A new smart-collection paper reports lower fuel use, with evidence too limited to promise comparable local savings.

Québec's deposit-return surveys need their denominators attached

On November 20, Consignaction released an Ad hoc Recherche Web-panel survey of 2,032 Québec adults conducted September 17–29. Respondents had to live within 5 km of a Consignaction return location, know the network and have returned deposit containers within the previous six months. The operator says 93% of surveyed users intend to return and three-quarters are satisfied or very satisfied overall. Its separate 89% figure combines satisfaction with neutral responses; it is not an 89% satisfaction rate. These are operator-commissioned survey results, not a measure of access for all Québec residents. Consignaction release and eligibility criteria.

For context, the FCEI's November 19 release describes a different Léger Web survey: 1,036 Québec adults interviewed November 6–9. Its detailed report puts long waits at 36% among respondents who had visited a return location in 2026, a subgroup of 553 respondents. That is not 36% of all Québec adults or of all visits. FCEI release; Léger report, methodology and question 3.

The results do not establish an improvement or deterioration between September and November. People can report a past problem and still be satisfied or intend to return. For municipal monitoring, keep access, experience and intention to participate as separate indicators, retaining each question's eligible population and reference period.

Lethbridge announced a temporary branch outlet after heavy snow

Lethbridge's November 20 notice announced a temporary reopening of its south yard-waste site for November 21–22, restricted to residential tree branches. Commercial vehicles and other yard waste were excluded. The north and west sites remained winterized after their November 15 seasonal closure; the city also identified its Waste & Recycling Centre as a year-round outlet. The exceptional weekend has now elapsed. The notice establishes the announced service response, not how much material was received or whether it reduced disposal elsewhere. City of Lethbridge.

For Banff/Canmore planning, this is a useful example of defining a narrow storm-debris service: specify eligible material, users and duration before evaluating demand.

Research spotlight: smart collection needs a measurable baseline

Pal and colleagues' paper, posted November 20, combines bin-level sensors, GIS routing and AI-assisted forecasting for Bishnupur, India. It reports fuel consumption of 92 versus 65 litres per day for conventional and technology-supported collection. However, the paper does not specify a dated monitoring interval or sample counts for that comparison, and acknowledges that training, awareness and other operational changes may contribute. Its ward-level waste series also contains projections rather than independently verified measurements. The reported comparison cannot isolate a technology effect or establish a Bow Valley saving. Full paper, methods and limitations; publisher record.

Worth acting on Before a local sensor pilot, define comparable routes and observation periods; record fuel, tonnes collected, missed services and equipment costs together. Treat the study as a planning framework, with local performance still to be demonstrated.
