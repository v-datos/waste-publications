The takeaway Inverness projects a reduction in household waste sent to landfill; its announcement is a useful reminder to keep projected reductions separate from measured outcomes when comparing municipal performance.

At a glance

  • Inverness, Québec, projects about 25 kg less household waste per person over two years; the notice does not establish which measures caused the reduction.
  • Ontario's RPRA clarifies that charging a tire recycling fee at sale does not, by itself, create a retailer take-back obligation.
  • A small UK trail study finds differences in litter detectability even when volunteer groups perform similarly.

Inverness projects lower household waste disposal

In a November 26 announcement, Inverness says its 2026 projections indicate a reduction in household waste of about 25 kg per person over two years. It describes nearly 20 tonnes less going to landfill, a 7% decrease. These are the municipality's projected figures, rather than a final audited annual result. The notice also promotes source reduction, reuse, sorting, deposit returns and home composting, including a free composter offer. Municipal announcement.

The notice provides no weighing series, population denominator or evaluation separating those measures' effects. Before a Banff/Canmore comparison, establish the waste stream, reference years, population basis and period completeness. The announcement does not demonstrate which intervention caused the decline or quantify transferable savings.

Ontario distinguishes tire sales fees from return-point obligations

RPRA's consumer FAQ, posted November 26, says retailers may recover tire recycling costs through a sales fee; RPRA neither requires that fee nor sets its amount. A retailer in a producer's public collection network cannot charge for, or refuse, end-of-life drop-offs of the materials that location accepts. A retailer outside that network has no obligation to accept used tires under this guidance. RPRA clarification.

For Ontario resident-facing directories, verify network participation and accepted materials before listing a return point. This clarification applies to Ontario; it announces no Alberta rule.

Research spotlight: similar volunteers can still miss the same litter

Scott-Dickins and colleagues' November 24 paper tested Trash Free Trails survey methods in a Welsh forest. The methods describe twenty volunteers in four groups of five by experience, scientific background and training, plus two detection-only participants. However, some figure captions give n=22 for other analyses, so analysis-specific totals are not consistently reported. The experiment used seeded litter on mock transects. Full study, methods and results.

The authors found no statistically significant group differences in survey accuracy, precision, detection or categorisation. Item visibility did differ: cigarettes and some other small items were harder to detect than bottles and cans. This small, single-site autumn experiment does not establish that training is unnecessary, and its detection differences are not correction factors validated for Bow Valley trails.

Worth acting on In a local trail-litter pilot, assess missed items and category confusion separately, and hold survey conditions consistent before interpreting changes in item counts as changes in litter abundance. Agreement between volunteer groups alone cannot rule out a shared detection bias.
